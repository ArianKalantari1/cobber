## Summary

- Classic dirty martinis (Difford's) carry brine at about 15-17% of the
  pre-dilution liquid, in a drink that is about 60 ml of spirit. The working
  spec (20 ml brine in 140 ml = 14.3% of the final volume) is in the same share
  of liquid as a classic after stirring. Its brine per ml of spirit is much
  higher.
- Published long and spritz-style builds put brine at roughly 9-10% of total
  liquid (Badger Liquor, Aisle Prompt, Dan Murphy's Dirty Spritz, all
  SEEN-IN-EXCERPT). The spec is above that range.
- The brine is the main salt source, so what matters is its NaCl %. No reliable
  figure for any bottled brand was found. One label-derived estimate is about
  4% w/v for Fee Brothers (DERIVED). The fermentation brine for Spanish-style
  olives is quoted at 9-10% w/v (SEEN-IN-EXCERPT). Packed-jar brines vary, so
  measure the house brine.
- The spec has the least gin of any benchmark (15 ml against 45-75 ml) and the
  most brine per ml of gin. The "dirty" signal rests on brine, olive garnish and
  vermouth, not on spirit weight. Test salinity and the gin dose first.

## 1. Classic specs

- Difford's gin version is the strongest source seen, but only via excerpt (table
  above). It is stirred and served up, so about 20-25% dilution from stirring
  would follow. That percentage is a general rule of thumb and UNVERIFIED here.
- Punch's house "standard" of 1/2 oz brine (about 15 ml) matches Difford's.
- Typical classic brine share is therefore 12-17% of pre-dilution liquid.
  DERIVED from the rows above.
- Typical salt level in the glass cannot be stated, because brine NaCl % is
  unknown (see section 3). Scenario arithmetic, DERIVED, assumptions marked:
  - Brine at 4% w/v NaCl (assumed from the Fee Brothers label below), 15 ml in
    about 120 ml after stirring: 0.6 g / 120 ml = 0.5% w/v, i.e. 5 g/L.
  - Spec drink, 20 ml of the same brine in 140 ml: 0.8 g / 140 ml = 0.57% w/v,
    i.e. about 5.7 g/L (ignoring salt in the sparkling or vermouth).
  - If the brine were 10% w/v, the same spec gives 1.43% (14 g/L), well above
    anything in the section 4 excerpts.

## 2. How bars build dirty and olive spritzes

- Builds found are in the table. Common pattern in the excerpts: dry vermouth
  base, 1/2 oz (15 ml) brine, 1-3 oz sparkling and/or soda, lemon, wine glass or
  highball.
- Punch's Timmons quote (excerpt): he wanted a spritz that was "bright and
  refreshing" with "a savory texture". SEEN-IN-EXCERPT.
- Punch's Highball swaps part of the vermouth for manzanilla sherry and uses
  only 1/4 oz brine, so salt comes partly from the sherry. Whether manzanilla
  carries salt is a flavour claim in the excerpt only.
- Spirit weight in the long drinks: 0 ml (vermouth-only), 45 ml (Badger), 75 ml
  (Atsu). The spec's 15 ml gin is lower than all of them.

## 3. Salt content of olive brines

All SEEN-IN-EXCERPT or DERIVED. No page opened.

- Spanish-style green olive fermentation brine: roughly 9-10% w/v NaCl; one
  source also shows 7-8% in studies; a USDA-hosted review says NaCl must be
  raised above 8.5% to stabilise olives after fermentation. Sources surfaced:
  https://ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle Pubs/p390.pdf,
  https://digital.csic.es/bitstream/10261/255823/1/fmicb-12-650754.pdf,
  https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2021.650754/pdf.
  These describe processing brines, not what is in a retail jar.
- Acidity: final acidity about 0.30-1.00% as lactic acid (USDA-hosted review
  excerpt). A 2003 study acidified brines to pH 4.0. High initial salt can keep
  pH from dropping below about 4.5. SEEN-IN-EXCERPT.
- Retail product label data (via retailer pages, excerpts only):
  - Fee Brothers olive brine: 240 mg sodium per 15 ml
    (https://odoo.qualifirst.com/food/liquid_flavouring_extracts/olive-brine-for-martinis-l).
    DERIVED: 16 g/L Na x 2.54 = about 40.6 g/L NaCl = about 4.1% w/v, if all
    sodium is NaCl (UNVERIFIED assumption).
  - Stirrings: "65 mg sodium", serving size unclear. Not usable.
- NOT FOUND: any NaCl % for Castelvetrano, Queen/Manzanilla/Gordal jar brine,
  Filthy, or Crawley's. Punch describes Manzanilla/Queen brine as salty and
  Castelvetrano as "less-salty" (excerpt), with no numbers.
- Estimating salinity in the bar:
  - Sourced: a brine hydrometer reads salt as % by weight at a reference
    temperature (Fisher H-B Durac, 15 C / 60 F). A retailer chart gives SG 1.020
    about 5% and SG 1.040 about 10% (percent by weight). Another source gives
    10% brine density as about 1.071 g/ml and salt (g) = water (g) x p / (100 - p).
    SEEN-IN-EXCERPT: https://www.dwk.com/kimble-salt-and-brine-hydrometer-0-264-sodium-chloride-52140-0264,
    https://sausagemaker.com/products/brine-tester.
  - Weighing 100 ml: density = mass / 100. Then % w/v is approximately % w/w x
    density. The conversion is DERIVED, with no source. Caveat: olive brine
    also holds acid, sugars and phenolics, so density overestimates NaCl. A
    salinity refractometer or salt meter is not shown suitable either:
    a conductivity meter was reported unsuitable for tomato juice and vinegary
    foods (Horiba excerpt), and an acidic brine is similar.
  - Most reliable for the bar: measure the house brine's sodium with a lab test
    or a brand-validated salt meter, or use chloride titration strips. UNVERIFIED
    recommendation.

## 4. Salt perception in drinks

- NaCl detection threshold in cream soup about 0.44-0.64 g/L (0.04-0.06%)
  (excerpt, bariatric study). Thresholds in tomato medium differ from water; tomato
  juice was reported to enhance perceived saltiness (excerpt).
- Grape juice and wine: thresholds rise with age; some consumers detect salt in
  wine below legal limits (excerpt).
- NOT FOUND: published NaCl % ranges for Bloody Mary, gose, tomato juice as a
  drink, or dirty martini. No "pleasantly savoury" range can be cited. The
  scenario figure of about 5-6 g/L above is not benchmarked against anything.

## 5. What drives olive brine flavour

- Fermentation produces lactic acid (main), acetic acid and ethanol, plus
  alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, esters (21 volatiles in one Greek-style study;
  excerpt only). Lactic bacteria lower pH. SEEN-IN-EXCERPT.
- Phenolics: oleuropein, hydroxytyrosol, tyrosol and verbascoside are analysis
  targets in brine papers (excerpt). Spanish-style olives are lye-treated
  (1.8-2.5% NaOH), which removes most bitterness before brining.
- NOT FOUND: any sourced odour descriptors, sensory thresholds, or key aroma
  compounds. Do not claim specific aroma molecules.
- Echo ingredients (caperberry brine, guindilla brine, manzanilla sherry,
  pastis, celery bitters, saline): no citation found for echoing olive brine
  flavour. Only Punch's use of manzanilla sherry in a dirty highball is
  SEEN-IN-EXCERPT (the claim of echo is not). A related Punch article exists at
  https://punchdrink.com/articles/dirty-martini-olive-brine-alternatives/ and
  should be read when punchdrink.com is allowed.

## 6. Dilution, bubbles and ice melt

- Carbonation: Cowart (1998) found CO2 added to taste solutions changed quality
  profiles, "particularly for sweet and salty tastes", with only small changes in
  total intensity. Direction for salt was not in the excerpt. SEEN-IN-EXCERPT:
  https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/889762817.
- Temperature: studies conflict on whether warming raises NaCl intensity
  (2026 preprint excerpt, https://www.bio.fsu.edu/vincislab/graphics/marques_et_al_2026.pdf).
- Dilution and ice melt: NOTHING found. Straight dilution lowers salt
  concentration proportionally (arithmetic), but perceived effect is unsourced.
